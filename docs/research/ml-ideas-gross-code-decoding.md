# Cut the doomed tail before adding models

> **Snapshot (Sun Nov 22, ~1 AM CT).** AI-assisted literature review plus a small local benchmark, written mid-hackathon to plan the last hours. Some conclusions changed after more data: the net-benefit router later beat the simple rule on fresh seeds at p = 0.005, the leg cap + OSD-0 turned out to be a speed/accuracy trade-off rather than free, and closer prior work on loss-aware relay-BP turned up (see [prior-work-and-novelty.md](prior-work-and-novelty.md)). Final numbers: [root README](../../README.md). Benchmark scripts and raw outputs: [`ml-ideas-benchmark/`](ml-ideas-benchmark/) (run from the repo root; 400 shots, p = 0.005, Z basis, two jobs on two cores).


The best use of the last 4-6 hours is not a new neural network. It is to stop paying for shots that are going to fail anyway. In a local benchmark at p=0.005 (Z basis), **all 11 of 400 shots on which Relay-BP-1 never converged were logical failures, and they used about 45% of its total decode time** ([local benchmark](ml-ideas-benchmark/out_p005_ns30.txt)). That gives three moves. First, a leg cap with an OSD-0 fallback on Relay's own posteriors, which fixes p99 at almost no cost in LER. Second, re-label the router to predict *rescue* rather than *failure*, and plot it against oracle bounds. Third, a time-boxed gradient-free tune of the Relay-BP memory strengths, which the Relay-BP authors list as an open question ([arXiv 2506.01779](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.01779v2)). The first two can mostly be done from cached per-shot data and add no latency. Published neural decoders that beat Relay-BP on the Gross code needed around 3×10⁸ training examples and up to 100 H200 GPU-hours ([arXiv 2604.08358](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.08358v1)), so they are slide context, not experiments. On the loss result: the core idea (prior 1/2 on heralded locations) is standard. The combination of Relay-BP, herald-indexed Stim DEM columns, a circuit-level Gross code, and per-shot-flag controls appears unpublished, and other authors explicitly name BP adaptation as future work ([arXiv 2412.07841](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.07841v3)). That is the novelty claim to make on slides. Confidence that it is unpublished is moderate, not high.

A note on evidence. The local benchmark used only 400 shots at one p (0.005) and one basis (Z), and it ran as **2 concurrent jobs on 2 cores**. Absolute timings are therefore inflated and noisy, and failure counts carry roughly ±4-5 failures of uncertainty. Use them to compare decoders, and re-time single-threaded before quoting any ms figure. The scripts are `bp_postprocessing_bench_full.py` and `bp_postprocessing_bench_capped.py` (arguments: `p shots n_bposd num_sets`), with raw outputs in `ml-ideas-benchmark/`. The `num_sets=5` run crashed while printing (zero BP-OSD shots requested). Before crashing it printed that 96/400 shots did not converge, plus the Relay-BP-1 and Relay-BP-5 results (19/400 each).

## Relay-BP-1's tail is almost pure waste, and a leg cap removes it (posterior post-processing)

The benchmark separates where Relay-BP-1's cost goes from where its errors come from. On the same 400 shots at p=0.005, Relay-BP-1 (authors' settings, about 300 legs) failed 19 times, at a mean of 342-420 ms and a p99 of 5.3-8.4 s. Relay-BP-5 also failed 19 times, at a mean of about 740-800 ms. Of the 19 Relay-BP-1 failures, 11 were shots that never converged; each took about 7 s and all were wrong. The other 8 converged to a wrong answer ([local benchmark](ml-ideas-benchmark/out_p005_ns30.txt)). With the number of legs capped (`num_sets`), the results were:

| Second stage on capped Relay posteriors | Fails/400 | Mean ms | p99 ms |
|---|---|---|---|
| `num_sets=0` (80-iteration memory BP only) | 135 | 14 | 36 |
| `num_sets=0` + OSD-0 | 82 | 32 | 387 |
| `num_sets=0` + OSD-CS10 | 63 | 166 | 743 |
| `num_sets=30` | 53 | 118 | 894 |
| `num_sets=30` + OSD-0 | 40 | 122 | 932 |
| `num_sets=30` + OSD-CS10 | 35 | 174 | 1606 |
| Relay-BP-1 uncapped | 19 | 342-420 | 5258-8383 |

Source: [local benchmark](ml-ideas-benchmark/out_p005_ns0.txt).

Two conclusions follow. First, **shots that converge between leg 30 and leg 300 are mostly decoded correctly by Relay and only partly rescued by OSD**, so the cap belongs well above 30. Somewhere around 100-200 legs should keep nearly all of Relay-BP-1's accuracy and cut off the 7-second doomed shots. Second, OSD-CS10 on Relay posteriors beats OSD-0 by only about 5 shots at about 3x the cost. **OSD-0 (about 50 ms per call on the 936×8,784 matrix, as measured) is the right bounded-cost fallback.** BP+LSD gave no speed advantage at this p (about 85 ms per LSD-0 call, because clusters are large), and as a standalone decoder it failed 87/400 ([local benchmark](ml-ideas-benchmark/out_p005_ns0.txt)). Using one decoder's soft output to order another's elimination has precedent in BP+OTF ([arXiv 2409.01440](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.01440)) and in Ambiguity Clustering ([arXiv 2406.14527](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.14527v2)). We found no paper that runs OSD on Relay-BP posteriors.

**Implementation.** `posterior_ratios` is LLR = log(P0/P1); in every converged shot checked it was negative on the bits set in `decoding` ([local benchmark](ml-ideas-benchmark/out_p005_ns30.txt)). ldpc 2.4.1 has no public "OSD on these LLRs" entry point, so the workaround is to build `BpOsdDecoder(H, error_channel=pri, max_iter=1, bp_method="minimum_sum", osd_method="OSD_0")` once and overwrite its channel probabilities for each shot:

```python
rc = relay_decoder(H, pri, stop_nconv=1, num_sets=K)        # kit wrapper over relay_bp
res = rc.decode_detailed(s)
e = res.decoding
if not res.success:
    llr = np.clip(np.nan_to_num(np.asarray(res.posterior_ratios, float)), -50, 50)
    osd0.update_channel_probs(np.clip(1/(1+np.exp(llr)), 1e-12, 1-1e-12))
    e = osd0.decode(s)
```

**Much of this can come from cached data.** With `stop_nconv=1`, Relay-BP-1 stops at its first converged leg. Its `iterations` count therefore tells you which leg it converged on (pre_iter=80 for leg 0, then 60 per later leg). A capped run gives exactly the same answer whenever `iterations ≤ 80 + 60·K`, provided the gamma draws are the same on every decode call. Verify that by decoding one syndrome twice. Shots above the cap can conservatively be counted as failures, which is close to true since every non-converged shot failed in the benchmark. That yields an LER-versus-cap curve, mean cost, and the fraction of shots over a real-time budget, all without new decoding. Only the OSD-0 rescue on capped shots needs fresh runs, and those are the few percent of shots that hit the cap. A useful slide metric: the IBM FPGA Relay-BP budget is about **333 BP iterations per window** at 24 ns per iteration ([arXiv 2510.21600](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.21600)). That is roughly leg 4, so the share of shots exceeding it is a hardware-meaningful tail statistic.

**Expected gain:** p99 latency down by several times, with LER within error bars of uncapped Relay-BP-1 if the cap is about 100 or more legs. **Time:** about 1 h on cached data, plus about 1 h of reruns on the capped subset. **Risk:** low. The main hazard is non-deterministic gamma sampling across calls, which would make the cached-data simulation approximate. **Fair evaluation:** use the same shots for every arm, compare paired failures (McNemar), report cost in BP iterations as well as CPU ms, and report the fraction of shots over 333 iterations. Two more fallbacks are worth naming but are lower priority. BP-SF reaches BP-OSD accuracy with parallel trials, and its maximum latency is as low as 18% of BP-OSD ([arXiv 2507.00254](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.00254v3)). PriorTilt reports up to **4.3x lower LER than Relay-BP** on BB144 at similar mean latency and has public code ([arXiv 2611.13373](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.13373)). Both target replacing Relay rather than bolting onto it, so treat them as stretch goals.

## The router ties random because few failures are rescuable (cascades and routing)

The benchmark also explains the router result: **Relay-BP-5 failed on exactly as many shots as Relay-BP-1 (19/400 each) at p=0.005** ([local benchmark](ml-ideas-benchmark/out_p005_ns0.txt)). With 400 shots this is weak evidence. If it holds on the team's larger cached set, a failure detector with high AUROC will mostly send shots to Relay-BP-5 that Relay-BP-5 cannot fix. Those are also the most expensive shots to escalate. A compute-matched random mix wastes less budget on them. At p=3×10⁻³ the Relay-BP paper found Relay-BP-9 at 300 iterations about 2x better than Relay-BP-1 ([arXiv 2506.01779](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01779v2)). So headroom exists at lower p, but it is modest.

The cheapest high-value analysis is to **change the label to rescue**: y = (Relay-BP-1 wrong) AND (Relay-BP-5 right). Then add two curves to the LER-versus-cost plot: an oracle-failure router and an oracle-rescue router. If the oracle-rescue curve sits close to random at a 2% budget, the tie is a ceiling, not a modelling failure. That is a strong slide. A cost-aware variant ranks shots by P(rescue)/E[cost], with cost predicted by a second regressor on the same features. This targets the observed problem that cost piles up on hard shots. The closest published analogue is IonQ's hybrid rule, which re-decodes only shots with *intermediate* convergence. It gave the largest suppression in their comparison while using 32-65% of 2R-LEC's mean iterations ([arXiv 2609.26958](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.26958)). The router can also be turned into an **early-abort classifier**: predict "will not converge within the cap" from posterior statistics and abort to OSD-0. That label is cleaner, because such shots are nearly certain failures ([local benchmark](ml-ideas-benchmark/out_p005_ns30.txt)).

For features, the strongest cheap signal in the literature is iteration count to first convergence. IonQ's M(s) cut Gross-code LER **78x at 0.1% rejection** at p=10⁻³ ([arXiv 2609.26958](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.26958)). The strongest moderately cheap signal is re-decode agreement under reweighted priors (2R-LEC). On the Gross code at p=0.1% it took Relay-BP LER from 2.20×10⁻⁷ to 1.60×10⁻⁸ ([arXiv 2601.17757](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.17757v1)). However, it adds at least one decode of latency, so it conflicts with the feature freeze. The mod-3 defect-count rule for BB codes ([arXiv 2604.07995](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.07995v1)) assumes weight-3 columns. It probably does not transfer to a circuit-level DEM, but it is a free feature to test. Note that all of these sharp numbers are at p=10⁻³. At p=0.3%, cluster-norm confidence needed 19% abort to reach 3 orders of magnitude ([arXiv 2510.05795](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05795v4)), so confidence is much blunter at the team's operating points.

The negative result has real slide value. A 2026 multi-institution review states that fair cascade comparisons should match accuracy and load and report latency distributions. It notes that many published results do not, and that confidence-gated cascades concentrate escalations in bursts ([arXiv 2611.16271](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.16271)). Fujitsu's Decoder Switching compares only against weak-alone and strong-alone and has no random baseline. It also explicitly proposes Relay-BP soft output for BB codes as future work ([arXiv 2510.25222](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.25222)). **We found no QEC cascade paper that compares against a compute-matched random mix.** The team's tie at p=0.004 is therefore a methodological contribution: a well-calibrated failure detector does not automatically beat chance at equal compute. For the possible ~5% win at p=0.005 and 2% budget, use a paired test on the same shots (McNemar on per-shot correctness, router versus random mix at matched budget) and bootstrap the difference. A 5% difference at a few percent per-shot failure needs thousands of failures to resolve, so report it as "consistent with" unless the CI excludes zero. A cost caveat specific to `relay_bp`: there is no warm start, so escalation pays Relay-BP-1 plus a full Relay-BP-5 rerun. If gamma draws are deterministic, Relay-BP-5 repeats Relay-BP-1's legs, and a hardware implementation could simply continue. Reporting marginal cost (iters_R5 − iters_R1) as a second accounting applies to the random mix too, so it changes absolute cost but not the comparison.

## Tuning Relay-BP's memory strengths is the only zero-latency ML lever with a plausible gain (BP-family improvements)

Relay-BP's own paper says the problem-dependent memory-strength distribution, including negative values, is "indispensable". It says the Gross-code interval [−0.24, 0.66] with γ0 = 0.125 was found by gradient-free optimization, and it names better selection as an open question. Its heatmaps at p=3×10⁻³ show a hotspot for intervals that include negative gammas ([arXiv 2506.01779](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.01779v2)). Evolutionary BP shows that differential evolution over a few dozen shared BP weights can pay off. On BB codes under code-capacity noise it raised the n=144 pseudo-threshold from 5.3% to 9.2%, though the paper is internally inconsistent about this number. Its circuit-level BB results cover only [[72,12,6]], and its optimization took about 5.6 h on an EPYC CPU ([arXiv 2512.18273](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.18273)). Neither paper shows learned parameters beating Relay-BP on the circuit-level Gross code, so **expect modest gains, perhaps 1.1-2x, and possibly none**.

**Implementation sketch (zero added latency, same decoder and new constants).** Tune center, width, and gamma0 (optionally alpha and set_max_iter) with Optuna TPE or CMA-ES at p=0.005, which has the most failures and is where qDither also tuned ([arXiv 2611.07144](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.07144)). Use a fixed shot set as common random numbers, and cap `num_sets` at about 100 so the doomed tail does not dominate wall clock:

```python
def objective(t):
    c, w = t.suggest_float("c", 0.0, 0.45), t.suggest_float("w", 0.5, 1.3)
    dec = relay_decoder(H, pri, stop_nconv=1, num_sets=100,
                        gamma0=t.suggest_float("g0", 0.05, 0.25),
                        gamma_dist_interval=(c - w/2, c + w/2))
    return n_failures(dec, TUNE_SHOTS)        # pool over 20 cores, paired shots
```

Start from center 0.21 and width 0.9. A lower-dimensional variant parametrizes `explicit_gammas` as a per-leg schedule (for example an annealed sequence with 3-5 parameters). It is novel, but there is no evidence it beats i.i.d. draws. Check whether the binding accepts per-column gamma vectors and what `alpha` controls (probably the min-sum scaling); the notes did not verify either. **Compute:** the benchmark implies roughly 0.2-0.4 s per shot for Relay-BP-1 at p=0.005, with inflated timings. So 10⁴ shots on 20 cores takes about 2-3 minutes per candidate, which allows about 40-60 candidates in 2 h. That is enough for 3 parameters, not for 6. **Fair evaluation:** re-score the best 2-3 candidates on fresh shots at p=0.003, 0.004, and 0.005 in both bases, and compare paired against the published interval. Tuning-set gains will be optimistic. **Risk:** medium. The objective is noisy, and a 10-20% gain may not survive the fresh-shot check.

Several neighbouring options are weaker for this window. Grouped prior scaling uses separate LLR scales for time-like (measurement) and space-like DEM columns. It is physically motivated by the temporal-degeneracy failure mode identified for BB codes ([arXiv 2610.31877](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31877)), but it is speculative and untested with Relay-BP. Learning priors from data cannot beat Stim's oracle DEM. NVIDIA's per-mechanism learning comes within 1.00-1.05x of the oracle on [[144,12,12]], which shows the oracle prior is the ceiling for physically true priors ([NVIDIA guide](https://nvidia.github.io/cudaq-qec/performance/nv_qldpc_noise_learning_user_guide.html)). Full gradient-trained neural BP ([arXiv 2308.08208](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08208)) means writing a PyTorch BP on a sparse 936×8,784 graph and would produce a decoder slower than Relay's Rust core, so it is a demo at most. Automorphism ensembles reach only BP-OSD-0 accuracy on circuit-level BB codes ([arXiv 2503.01738](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01738)), and Relay's chained legs already beat independent ensembling: (7±1)×10⁻⁶ at about 331 iterations versus (1.4±0.2)×10⁻⁵ at about 578 ([arXiv 2506.01779](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.01779v2)). Two very recent decoders are worth naming on slides. TEMPO-BP adapts per-variable memory online, has public code, and claims Relay-BP-5 parity with 10-15 shallow instances ([arXiv 2611.21086](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21086)). qDither reports roughly Relay parity at p=3×10⁻³ with about 2% of the multiplications ([arXiv 2611.07144](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.07144)). Both are days to weeks old and not independently checked. The largest known lever is not ML: correlated XYZ decoding improves on X/Z-split Relay-BP-5 by about an order of magnitude at p=3×10⁻³ ([arXiv 2506.01779](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.01779v2)). The team decodes each basis separately, so this belongs under "limitations and future work", not tonight's work.

## Herald-reweighted Relay-BP is new as a combination, standard as a principle (erasure/loss-aware decoding)

Erasure-aware decoding is well established. Erasure conversion raised the surface-code threshold from 0.937% to 4.15% ([arXiv 2201.03540](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.03540)). Per-shot DEM reweighting for atom loss improved surface-code logical error by up to about 2.8 orders of magnitude at d=11, and its authors explicitly say it could be adapted to BP ([arXiv 2412.07841](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.07841v3)). For BB codes, Pandey sets erased qubits to prior 0.5 and the rest to 10⁻¹⁰ for BP-OSD, but under code-capacity noise only, and lists circuit level as a limitation ([arXiv 2603.19062](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19062v3)). Pecorari and Pupillo simulated the Gross code with circuit-level heralded gate erasures and BP-OSD, finding a threshold of about 0.7% → 1.2% and concluding that erasure information mainly lowers LER for BB codes ([arXiv 2502.20189](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.20189v2)). That conclusion matches the team's 2-5x. Delayed-erasure decoding is the experimental norm at Harvard/QuEra, where loss information plus an NN decoder gave 1.73x ([arXiv 2506.20661](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.20661)). Infleqtion's own 2025 paper post-selects on loss and leaves "more sophisticated decoders that account for the underlying physical circuit" to future work ([arXiv 2509.13247](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.13247v1)). That is the natural hook for an Infleqtion-hosted hackathon.

**Slide framing (no new experiments).** Claim novelty for four things. First, erasure-informed Relay-BP; we found no paper on it. Second, the herald-column Stim DEM construction, with the Pauli-injection check that the four branches collapse to "no effect plus one column per basis", which makes prior 1/2 exact in a CSS-split DEM. Third, the shuffled-flag and averaged-prior controls showing the gain needs each shot's own flags; few loss papers report this ablation. Fourth, the Gross-code atom-loss framing. Do not claim the 1/2-prior idea itself. Two caveats belong on the slide. The model heralds data qubits at the start of each cycle with perfect location and immediate replacement, which is the optimistic "prompt erasure" end of the spectrum. Real loss also hits ancillas and occurs mid-cycle. The 1% per-cycle stress point is realistic: Atom Computing measured about 0.24-0.27% loss per two-qubit gate and about 5×10⁻⁴ per move ([arXiv 2411.11822](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.11822v3)). Our estimate of 0.6-2% per cycle for a Gross-code data qubit is an inference from those per-operation rates, not a measured number.

**Cheap experiments if time remains (zero latency, Stim reruns only).** One is a fired-herald prior sweep (0.3-0.5). Exact Bayes says 1/2, but BP on a loopy graph sometimes prefers skewed priors, so a flat curve is itself a robustness result. The other is herald noise: flip flags with a false-negative or false-positive rate of 5-20% and show the gain degrades gracefully. Each takes about 30-60 min. The ML twist people will ask about is a small model mapping (flags + local syndrome) to per-column priors to handle imperfect or delayed heralds. It has no BB-code precedent; the closest is Tsinghua's GNN inferring loss on d=5 surface codes without flags ([arXiv 2604.14269](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.14269v2)). That makes it the right future-work item, not a 6-hour one.

## Transformers and GNNs need 10⁸ samples to beat Relay-BP here (neural decoders)

The published record is decisive for this window. The MIT recurrent transformer trained on about 3.8×10⁸ examples only matched BP-OSD-3 on [[144,12,12]] for p=0.3-0.7% and was about 7x worse at p=0.1% ([arXiv 2504.13043](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.13043)). Harvard's Cascade CNN does beat Relay-BP, by about 17x at p=0.1%, using BB group convolutions. It needed about 3×10⁸ examples and under 100 H200 GPU-hours, and its code is unreleased ([arXiv 2604.08358](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.08358v1)). AlphaQubit 2 has no qLDPC results ([arXiv 2512.07737](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.07737)). Astra-style learned message passing has BB results only under code capacity and took up to 24 h on an RTX 3080 ([arXiv 2408.07038](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.07038v2)). The one that fits a 3060 is Carty et al.'s 115k-parameter GNN on the DEM graph with BP posteriors as fault features. It is a failure predictor: on BB144 it gives 2.9x LER suppression at 1% rejection and 738x at 10% ([arXiv 2610.00504](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.00504v1)). At the team's 2% routing budget, even that architecture's suppression is small, and its base decoder was BP+LSD, not Relay-BP. Training it in 2 h is plausible, but it would replace the existing classifier at the cost of about 0.6 ms of GPU inference per shot, and the rescue ceiling above still binds. **Verdict:** do not start this before the 8 AM freeze. Cite the scale numbers on a "why not end-to-end ML" slide.

## Conclusion

The three picks are, in order: (1) the leg cap with OSD-0 fallback, (2) the rescue-label re-analysis with oracle bounds, and (3) a time-boxed gamma/alpha search. Run them in that order and stop the third if it is not paying off by mid-morning. The first two reframe the ML story in a way that holds up well. They say the cost of Relay-BP on the Gross code sits in a small set of unrescuable shots, so the right job for a learned model is deciding when to give up, not when to try harder. They also say a compute-matched random baseline is the comparison most of the cascade literature has skipped. Pick 4 (herald-noise robustness) is the best swap for pick 3 if the team would rather harden the headline than chase a small LER gain. The 30-minute slide task is worth doing regardless.

The one future-work idea that best ties the pieces together is a **learned herald-to-prior model**. A small network would map each shot's imperfect or delayed loss flags, plus the local syndrome, to per-column Relay-BP priors. It would extend the exact prompt-erasure result to the delayed, noisy heralds that real neutral-atom hardware produces. Harvard, Perrin et al., and Infleqtion each point toward this, and nobody has done it for a BB code.
